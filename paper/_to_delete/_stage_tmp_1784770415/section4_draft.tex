\section{Experiments}

\subsection{The Automated Frontier}

We first establish what automation resolves. The portfolio spans eight
configurations of the strongest off-the-shelf provers: Vampire~5.0.1 in five
modes (proof search and saturation, under Knuth-Bendix and lexicographic path
orderings) \citep{kovacs2013first}, the E prover in a proof and a saturation
mode \citep{schulz2019faster}, and Twee~2.6.1, an implementation of unfailing
completion built for equational reasoning \citep{smallbone2021twee}, each run
over per-prover budgets from 30 to 600 seconds. A law counts as resolved when
any configuration returns a verdict, and a claimed model or triviality proof
is one the tool itself reports; versions and flags are pinned in a released
container image. Finite-model finders such as Mace4 and Paradox are omitted
by theorem rather than by choice: admissibility certifies that no nontrivial
finite model exists, so they cannot succeed. Saturation and completion are
the automated methods able to attempt infinite-model construction at all.

Sweeping the portfolio over the admissible pool produces two populations
with almost nothing between them: laws that resolve nearly instantly, and
laws that do not resolve at all. Every one of the 262 Austin models the
screening pass constructs arrives in a median of 0.1 seconds; the full
sweep of the 4,141-law residual recovers only a thin further layer —
[FILL: 44] late triviality proofs and [FILL: 4] completion-only models,
about [FILL: 3]\% of laws swept, reassigned to their proper tiers by the
two-sided design — and nothing else, at any budget. The [FILL: final] laws
that remain are the hard tier, and the boundary is not a threshold we chose:
it is a gap in the distribution.

The floor is not without content. Among the laws the portfolio does resolve
is an order-five law and its dual (12857 and 33436) that the Equational
Theories Project recorded as having only trivial finite models while leaving
the existence of an infinite model unresolved. Our pipeline confirms the pair
Austin by two independent routes — a Vampire saturation and a Twee completion
— and the extracted 27-rule model is exercised directly: every rule fires,
and $x = y$ is refuted in it. The completed system is not orientable into a
plain rewrite system, which is precisely the ground-confluence obstacle that
keeps such models outside Lean's reach today; the full worked example is in
the appendix. The pipeline, pointed at the literature, returns a new fact —
but the portfolio reaches it, so the case marks the floor's edge, not the
frontier.

If difficulty were graded by compute, added budget would buy resolutions
along that gradient. It does not (Table~\ref{tab:curve}). On a 120-law
sample re-attacked with all eight configurations, three laws resolve at the
30-second rung — all trivial — and none at any higher rung, out to roughly
$170\times$ the screening pass's per-law time; a separate retry of 294
unresolved laws at ten times the budget converts 3.7\%, all to trivial,
none to a model. A law yields in fractions of a second or resists
everything we run: the difficulty is bound by method, not budget.

Nor is the difficulty one prover's blind spot. Superposition and completion
explore the space differently enough that each resolves instances the other
diverges on — Twee completes in seconds an order-five law whose saturation
runs Vampire to hundreds of clauses, and the sweep's recovered models are
completion-only. Yet the two paradigms' reach ends in the same place: past
the shed contamination, neither constructs a model the other misses, and the
residual resists both. The frontier is a property of the laws, not of a
tool.

It is also not a fixed target. Normalizing by the number of laws screened,
the yield of Austin laws rises with order (Figure~\ref{fig:density}): 20.5,
24.4, and 25.4 per thousand screened at orders six, seven, and eight. The
generator therefore mints frontier instances faster than any fixed method
consumes them, and evaluation can always draw on laws created after the
system under test was built — the benchmark cannot be exhausted from either
end.

\subsection{The LLM Frontier}

Language models enter ALPS on the same terms as the portfolio: nothing in the
task references how an answer is produced, only that it certifies. We
evaluate them under a fixed protocol designed so that failures are
attributable. Every model receives identical inputs — the law, the task
statement, and a fixed answer format — and interacts with the judge as a
verification oracle over three feedback rounds. On the trivial side the
formalization burden is removed entirely: the model emits only a chain of
terms, each step one application of the law; the harness matches each step,
bridges short gaps, and assembles the Lean proof itself, so the test is
finding the derivation, not writing Lean. Support is graded — runs range
from the bare law to full reveal, where prover-derived waypoint lemmas are
included in the prompt — and the harness is frozen across models. Saturation
provers are withheld from the models, since with them a model would simply
re-run the portfolio at larger budget; the judge and assembler are exactly
the tools any solver built on ALPS would use.

Instance selection is the step we control most carefully. From the trivial
tier we isolate a \emph{certified-easy} set of 63 laws: those whose complete
collapse chains a blind bidirectional search finds in under five seconds,
most only three law applications long. Each carries a privately held,
machine-found solution, and one such chain, driven through the assembler and
the Lean kernel, serves as an end-to-end positive control. A zero on this
set cannot be blamed on the instances — the answers exist, are short, and
are found by uninformed search almost instantly. On the construction side we
draw a 25-law sample of the hard tier; there the model proposes a
presentation $E$ and the ATP certificate of the verification section grades
it.

Table~\ref{tab:llm} reports the outcomes by the question each block
answers. On the certified-easy set at full support, o3 at medium reasoning
effort produces nine kernel-verified solves — to our knowledge the first
machine-checked language-model solutions to ALPS instances — while GPT-4.1
and o4-mini produce none on the same 63 laws under the identical frozen
protocol. The nine solutions are chains of three or four law applications,
most found on the second or third feedback round at a median of roughly
50{,}000 completion tokens per solved law: the verification oracle does real
work, and the capability tracks reasoning tier.

Ablating o3 on the nine laws it solves locates what the solves depend on.
With the waypoint lemmas withheld, four of the nine survive — the model
finds some short derivations entirely unaided — and with reasoning effort
reduced to low, none do. Solves exist only toward the top of both dials,
and rejected chains fail overwhelmingly the same way at every setting: they
propose steps that are not instances of the law at all, the failure that
eliminates the weaker models outright.

The construction side stays at zero, and the way it stays there is the
finding. In 23 of 25 attempts, the presentation o3 proposes entails the law
— the correctness query passes — but fails nontriviality: the proposed
structure collapses to a point, or its saturation does not terminate. The
remaining two fail the converse way, consistent but too weak to force the
law. Every attempt satisfies one horn of the certificate; none satisfies
both. Inventing a structure strong enough to entail the law yet loose
enough to keep two elements apart is precisely the synthesis the benchmark
isolates, and nothing we ran — prover, completion, or reasoning model —
achieves it. The asymmetry of the task thus reappears one level up: the
deductive side yields, modestly and under support, to the strongest
reasoning model we test; the construction side yields to nothing.
